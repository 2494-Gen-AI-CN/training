## Real-world framing and deliberate deviations

On a machine-learning team a model starts in a notebook: a data scientist frames the problem with the people who will use the model, pulls a sample of the team's data into Colab, Vertex AI Workbench or JupyterLab, puts a test set aside, tries a network, watches the training and validation curves in TensorBoard or an experiment tracker, reads the confusion matrix and the wrong examples with the people who own the decision (a missed fraud case costs more than a false alarm), changes one thing at a time and records each run. A model worth keeping moves into a reviewed Python package with tests, trains in a scheduled job on the team's own data with a pinned environment, saves its weights to a model registry and is served behind an endpoint. Recommenders at a shop or a streaming service use matrix factorisation or deep recommenders; autoencoders flag unusual transactions or sensor readings by their reconstruction error; support desks route tickets with text classifiers. The week's five builds are the exploring half of that work, each on a small public dataset so every result can be seen and checked, and Project 1 asks each learner to do the same on a dataset of their own.
