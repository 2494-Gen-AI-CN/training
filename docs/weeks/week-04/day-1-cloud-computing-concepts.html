It teaches cloud computing from first principles: the NIST definition and its five characteristics, regions and zones, the IaaS, PaaS and SaaS service models, the deployment models, the challenges of adoption, how pricing works, and the common Google Cloud services matched with those of AWS and Azure. Definition

What cloud computing is: one definition and five characteristics

Week 3 ended with the ticket desk running in a Docker container called desk-mysql on your own laptop. You started it, it stores its rows on your disk, and it stops when the laptop sleeps. That setup is the reference point for everything on this page, because cloud computing is the other way of getting the same database.

The definition in one sentence

The definition most teams and exams quote comes from the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST). It calls cloud computing a model for on-demand network access to a shared pool of configurable computing resources that can be rapidly provisioned and released with minimal management effort.1

In plain words, you rent computers, storage and software from a provider over a network. You ask for them when you need them, give them back when you are done, and pay for what you used. The provider owns the buildings, the machines and the people who keep them running.

The same NIST document names three service models and four deployment models.1 Step 3 covers the service models (who manages which layer) and step 4 the deployment models (who owns the infrastructure and who shares it). This step covers the five characteristics that make something a cloud at all.

The five essential characteristics

NIST lists five essential characteristics.1 A service that lacks one of them is hosting, or a server in someone else's room, and not cloud computing in the NIST sense. Each is compared with the desk on your laptop.

  • On-demand self-service means you get a resource yourself, through a web page or a command, without asking a person at the provider. The desk needed you to install Docker and start a container by hand.
  • Broad network access means the resource is reached over the network from ordinary devices such as laptops and phones. The desk answers only on your own laptop.
  • Resource pooling means the provider serves many customers from one shared pool of machines, a multi-tenant model, and you do not choose the exact machine.1 The desk has one machine and one tenant.
  • Rapid elasticity means capacity grows and shrinks quickly with the load, sometimes automatically. The desk's CPU, memory and disk are whatever your laptop has.
  • Measured service means usage is metered, monitored and reported, which is what makes paying per use possible.1 Nothing meters the desk.

Count them up and the desk on your laptop meets none of the five. A database that a provider runs for you meets all five: you create it from a form, reach it over the network, share the provider's pooled machines, resize it on request and see its use itemised on a bill.

You recognise these characteristics on sight in a provider's console. A "Create instance" button that returns a running machine within a minute is on-demand self-service. A billing page that lists hours, gigabytes and queries by day is measured service.

Virtualisation, the trick underneath

Resource pooling rests on virtualisation. Software on one physical server splits it into several virtual machines, each with its own operating system, and the provider hands those out to different tenants. You rent a slice of a machine, and the slice behaves like a whole computer.

An interviewer often follows the definition by asking where elasticity stops. Capacity is not unlimited: each account has quotas, and a region has only so much hardware, so a request can still be refused.2 A team that expects a big spike asks for a higher quota ahead of time.

Takeaways
  • Cloud computing is renting computing resources over a network, on demand, paid for by measured use.
  • NIST's five characteristics are self-service, network access, pooling, elasticity and measured service.
  • Virtualisation lets one physical server serve many tenants, which makes pooling possible.
  • Elasticity is limited by account quotas and by a region's hardware.
Say it in an interview

Cloud computing is getting computing resources from a provider over the network when I need them, and giving them back when I don't, paying only for what I used. NIST names five characteristics: on-demand self-service, broad network access, resource pooling, rapid elasticity and measured service.

A quick example: a MySQL container on my laptop has none of those five. A database a provider runs for me, created from a console form and billed by the hour, has all five.

Regions

Regions and zones: where a provider's computers actually sit

"The cloud" sounds placeless, yet every rented machine sits in a building in a named place. Providers group those buildings into regions and zones, and the two words come up in almost every cloud interview.

A region holds zones

In Google Cloud a region is an independent geographic area, and each region is made of zones. A zone is a deployment area inside a region.3 The region for Toronto is northamerica-northeast2, and this week's BigQuery work stays in it.3

The zones of one region are separate failure domains linked by low-latency networking.3 Each zone has its own power and networking, so a fault in one does not take down the others. AWS uses the names Region and Availability Zone for the same idea, and Azure says region and availability zone.4

RegionZone (availability zone)
What it isA geographic area such as TorontoAn isolated deployment area inside one region
ContainsSeveral zonesMachines with their own power and networking
Chosen forNearness to users and where the data may liveSurviving one zone's failure, with copies in other zones
Named in rulesData-residency rules name a region or a countryRarely; zones are an availability choice

A common mix-up treats the two words as the same thing, or calls a zone a country. A region is the place, a zone is one isolated part of that place, and one region always holds several zones.

Why a service spreads over zones

Resources placed in several zones of one region survive the failure of a single zone. That is why managed services spread their copies over zones without being asked.3 A service kept in one zone goes down with that zone.

Picture three zones in one region, each holding a copy of a service, and zone 1 losing power. A service kept only in zone 1 is gone until the power returns. A service with copies in all three zones answers from zone 2 while zone 1 is down, and most users never notice.

Real services do this with replicas and automatic failover, and the customer sees at most a short pause. A replica is a copy of the service or its data kept in another zone, and failover is the automatic switch to that copy when the first one stops answering.

The limit is that zones share a region. They guard against one building failing, not against a problem that affects the whole region, and they do nothing for users who are far away. Latency grows with the distance between a user and the region, so choosing the region is a design decision.3

You recognise a region on sight as a value in a form, a command or a config file under a name such as region or location. A value like northamerica-northeast2 tells you where the data lives and which residency rules it can meet.

Takeaways
  • A region is a geographic area; a zone is an isolated deployment area inside it.
  • Zones in one region have separate power and networking and fast links between them.
  • Spreading copies over zones survives one zone failing; it does not cover a whole region.
  • Toronto is northamerica-northeast2, the region the desk's warehouse uses this week.
Say it in an interview

A region is a geographic area where a provider has data centres, like Toronto, and an availability zone is an isolated part of that region with its own power and networking. A region holds several zones linked by fast networks.

I'd put copies of a service in more than one zone so that losing one zone doesn't take it down, and I'd pick the region for where the users are and where the data is allowed to live.

Service types

Service types: IaaS, PaaS and SaaS, and what each one buys you

"As a service" means the provider runs part of the technology stack for you and sells the result as a metered service. The three service models differ in how much of the stack the provider runs. They stack on each other: IaaS gives you machines, PaaS adds a runtime or a managed platform on top, and SaaS delivers a finished application.1

The desk hosted three ways

Picture the ticket desk moved off your laptop in three different ways.

  • A virtual machine from Compute Engine, Google's IaaS service, with MySQL installed on it by you.5 In IaaS the customer provisions processing, storage and networks, and controls the operating system and the applications, but not the infrastructure underneath.1
  • Cloud SQL, a managed relational database service for MySQL, PostgreSQL and SQL Server. Google runs the patching, the backups and the failover.6 That is the PaaS shape: you bring the schema and the queries.
  • A hosted help-desk product that your support staff open in a browser. In SaaS the customer uses the provider's application and manages nothing beyond a few settings.1

Draw the desk as a stack of six layers and mark who runs each one under each model. The boundary between the provider and you climbs two layers per model.

Layer, from the bottomOn-premisesIaaSPaaSSaaS
Hardware and networkyouproviderproviderprovider
Virtual machineyouproviderproviderprovider
Operating systemyouyouproviderprovider
MySQL engine, patches, backupsyouyouproviderprovider
Schema and queriesyouyouyouprovider
Help-desk applicationyouyouyouprovider

Architecture diagrams draw the same thing as a responsibility-stack table, with a column each for on-premises, IaaS, PaaS and SaaS and rows from networking up to data. Wherever you meet one, read it by finding the row where "provider" turns into "you".

IaaS, PaaS, SaaS: the benefit each one buys

Each model buys the team something different, and each one gives up some control in return.

ModelBenefit to the teamThe team still managesUsually billed
IaaS (Compute Engine)No hardware to buy, machines in minutesOS patches, the database, backupsPer virtual machine hour
PaaS (Cloud SQL)Patches, backups and failover done for itSchema, queries, usersPer instance, with the engine managed
SaaS (hosted help desk)Nothing to install or run, updates shipped by the vendorIts data and a few settingsPer user seat

The IaaS and PaaS rows follow the Compute Engine and Cloud SQL documentation, and the SaaS row follows NIST.561 The billing column is how you recognise a model on a pricing page.

The more the provider manages, the less control the customer keeps over versions, tuning and placement.1 SaaS is the fastest to adopt, but the customer cannot change the product's data model or where it is hosted.

The usual mistake is calling Cloud SQL IaaS because a MySQL server runs inside it. The test is what the team still manages. Nobody on the team patches Cloud SQL's operating system, so it is not IaaS.

The shared responsibility model

Interviewers often ask "what do I still manage?" as a question about security. AWS's shared responsibility model splits it in two: AWS secures the cloud itself, and the customer secures what they put in it.7 The line sits lower in IaaS and higher in SaaS, and it never disappears.

In every model the customer still decides who may read the data. A storage bucket left open to the public is the customer's error under this model.7

Serverless: where it sits

Serverless services hide the servers completely and bill by use. Cloud Run, which runs containers, is one.8 BigQuery, Google's serverless analytics database, is another.9 Neither asks you to choose a machine size.

When an interviewer asks whether BigQuery is IaaS, PaaS or SaaS, the safe answer places it with PaaS at the serverless end. You bring tables and SQL, Google runs everything else, and you never see a server.

Takeaways
  • IaaS rents machines, PaaS adds a managed platform, SaaS delivers a finished application.
  • The test for the model is what the team still manages, not what runs inside.
  • More provider management means less control over versions, tuning and placement.
  • Under shared responsibility the customer always owns its data and who can access it.
Say it in an interview

IaaS gives me virtual machines, so I skip buying hardware but still patch the OS and run the database. PaaS, like Cloud SQL, runs patches, backups and failover for me, so I only manage the schema and queries. SaaS is a finished application billed per user, with nothing to install at all.

The more the provider manages, the faster I move and the less I can tune. Whatever the model, my data and who can access it stay my responsibility.

Deployment

Deployment models: public, private, hybrid and multi-cloud

Service models answer "who manages which layer". Deployment models answer a different question: who owns the infrastructure, and who else shares it.

The four models

NIST defines public cloud as infrastructure open to the general public and owned by a provider, and private cloud as infrastructure that serves a single organisation.1 What makes it private is that one organisation uses it.

Hybrid cloud, in NIST's words, is two or more distinct infrastructures bound together by technology that lets data and applications move between them.1 The usual shape is an organisation's own servers joined to a public cloud. NIST's list has a fourth model, the community cloud, but interviews mostly ask about the four in the table below.

Multi-cloud is not one of the NIST four. It means using services from more than one provider, so it is defined by usage, not by ownership.10

ModelWho owns the infrastructureWho shares itTypical reason
PublicA providerAny customer who signs upStart fast, pay as you grow
PrivateOne organisationThat organisation onlyControl and regulation
HybridThe organisation for one part, a provider for the otherJoined so work and data can moveKeep some data on site, use the cloud for the rest
Multi-cloudTwo or more providersEach provider's other customersAvoid depending on one provider, or use each one's strengths

People often confuse hybrid and multi-cloud. Hybrid joins your own or private infrastructure to a public cloud. Multi-cloud uses more than one public provider. An organisation can be both at once.

A situation that suits each

Three facts about an organisation usually settle the model: whether it keeps its own site, whether it uses a public cloud, and how many providers it uses. Real decisions also weigh cost, skills and regulation. Each case below is a situation an interviewer might give you.

  • A startup owns no servers and cannot guess its load, so the public cloud lets it pay only for what it uses and grow on demand.
  • A bank's core ledger is the record of every account balance. Regulators and the bank want full control over where it runs and who touches it, so it often stays in a private cloud.
  • A hospital keeps patient records on its own servers but sends extra work to a public cloud at peaks. Running a steady load on site and sending the overflow out is called bursting.10
  • A firm that runs on two providers can move if one raises prices or has an outage. That hedge is the classic multi-cloud reason.

Hybrid and multi-cloud add integration and skills cost, because every extra environment needs its own tooling and people who know it.10

You recognise the models on sight in architecture diagrams. An on-premises box joined to a provider box by a VPN, an encrypted link over the internet, is hybrid. Two provider logos around one application is multi-cloud.

Data residency and region choice

The follow-up question is usually why the bank or the hospital keeps data where it does. The answer is data residency: a rule, from a law or a contract, that certain data must be stored in a named country or on the organisation's own premises.

A public cloud can meet many such rules through region choice. In BigQuery, choosing a location such as Toronto keeps the stored data in that region.11 When a rule asks for the organisation's own building, only a private or hybrid setup meets it.

Takeaways
  • Public cloud is a provider's, shared with anyone; private cloud serves one organisation.
  • Hybrid joins your own infrastructure to a public cloud; multi-cloud uses several providers.
  • Hybrid and multi-cloud add integration and skills cost for each extra environment.
  • Data residency rules often decide the model, and region choice meets many of them.
Say it in an interview

Public cloud is a provider's infrastructure shared by any customer, which suits a startup that wants to pay as it grows. Private cloud serves one organisation, like a bank keeping its core ledger under its own control.

Hybrid joins on-site systems to a public cloud, say a hospital that keeps records on site and bursts to the cloud at peaks. Multi-cloud means using more than one provider, often to avoid depending on one. The last two both cost extra in integration and skills.

Challenges

Challenges of adopting the cloud, and the plan for each

Renting instead of owning removes some problems and creates others. An interviewer who asks about the challenges of cloud adoption wants each problem named together with the plan an organisation makes for it.

Five challenges and five plans

ChallengeWhat goes wrongThe usual plan
Cost controlMetered use grows quietly until the monthly bill surprises someoneBudgets with alerts, labels per project, a regular bill review
Security and complianceA setting left open exposes data, or data sits in the wrong countryClear ownership of access settings, data kept in a named region such as Toronto
Vendor lock-inLeaving a provider becomes slow and expensiveOpen formats for the data, a written exit plan
LatencyUsers far from the region wait for every answerChoose the region near the users, measure response times
SkillsThe team knows servers but not the provider's servicesTraining before the move

Security and compliance lean on the shared responsibility model from step 3. The provider secures its buildings and machines, but the customer still configures who can access what, so a public bucket is the customer's error.7

Compliance often means data residency. Choosing a BigQuery location such as Toronto keeps the stored data in that region, which supports a rule that Canadian data stays in Canada.11 Organisations back the choice with a policy that lists the regions resources may use.

Latency is the time an answer takes to arrive. It grows with the distance between the user and the region, so region choice is a design decision made early.3

Cost control: budgets and alerts

A budget in Cloud Billing is an amount you expect to spend, with alert thresholds set as percentages of it. When spending crosses a threshold, the account owner is notified. The budget does not by itself stop the spending.12

The sketch below replays five days of spending against a budget of 100, with three example thresholds. The test before < limit <= spent is a chained comparison, true only on the day the total first reaches the limit, so each alert fires once.

BUDGET = 100
THRESHOLDS = [50, 90, 100]   # percent of the budget
daily_spend = [20, 25, 15, 30, 20]

spent = 0
day = 0
for cost in daily_spend:
    day = day + 1
    spent = spent + cost
    for percent in THRESHOLDS:
        before = spent - cost
        if before < BUDGET * percent / 100 <= spent:
            print(f"day {day}: alert, {spent} spent crosses {percent}%")
print(f"after day {day}: {spent} spent against a budget of {BUDGET}")
day 3: alert, 60 spent crosses 50%
day 4: alert, 90 spent crosses 90%
day 5: alert, 110 spent crosses 100%
after day 5: 110 spent against a budget of 100

Three alerts went out and the spending still passed the budget, because an alert only sends a message. A team that wants spending to stop has to act on the alert, by hand or with automation it builds.

You recognise cost control on sight as a budget alert email that names a project and a percentage, or a billing report grouped by project.

Lock-in, egress and open formats

Lock-in has a concrete mechanism behind it. Data leaving a provider's network, called egress, is billed separately from compute and storage.13 Moving a large store of data to another provider therefore costs money as well as time.

The plan is to keep data in open file formats that any tool can read, which Tuesday's page names, and to avoid tying every step to one provider's special features. The trade-off is that those features are often what make a service productive. BigQuery's SQL, for instance, is Google's own dialect, GoogleSQL.9 Teams usually accept some lock-in for the productivity and plan the exit for their data.

Takeaways
  • The five challenges are cost, security and compliance, lock-in, latency and skills.
  • Budgets send alerts at thresholds; they do not stop spending by themselves.
  • Region choice serves both data residency and latency.
  • Egress fees make leaving costly; open formats keep the exit possible.
Say it in an interview

The main challenges are cost control, security and compliance, vendor lock-in, latency and skills. For cost I'd set budgets with alerts and label resources by project, remembering an alert doesn't stop spending. For compliance I'd pick a Canadian region like Toronto and restrict which regions resources may use, and I'd own the access settings, since the provider doesn't.

For lock-in I'd keep data in open formats and know what egress would cost. For latency I'd choose a region near the users, and for skills I'd train the team before the move.

Pricing

Pricing: how the bill is built, and a worked estimate

Measured service, the fifth characteristic from step 1, is what makes a cloud bill possible. This step covers how the bill is built and how to estimate one before it arrives. Prices change, so the figures here are the ones the sources gave on the date at the top of the page; check the live pricing page before you rely on one.

Pay-as-you-go, committed use and free tiers

Providers sell the same resources under a few pricing models.

  • Pay-as-you-go bills metered usage with no commitment. You stop using a resource and the charges stop.14
  • Reserved or committed use trades a one- or three-year commitment for a lower price than on-demand. Google calls these committed use discounts.14
  • Free tiers give a monthly allowance at no charge. Google's Free Program has two parts: a 90-day free trial with a one-off credit for new accounts, and the Free Tier, monthly allowances on some products that do not expire with the trial.17

Committed use has a trade-off worth naming in an interview. It is cheaper per unit, but it charges whether or not the capacity is used.14 It suits a steady load you can predict, and pay-as-you-go suits a load you cannot.

The four meters

Most cloud bills come down to four meters.

  • Compute: the time a machine runs, so a pricing page for virtual machines bills per hour.
  • Storage: data kept, billed per GiB a month. BigQuery's first 10 GiB a month are free.15
  • Queries: work done on request. BigQuery's on-demand analysis is billed per TiB of bytes processed, and the first 1 TiB each month is free.15
  • Egress: data leaving the provider's network, billed separately from the other three.13

A GiB is 1024 to the power 3 bytes and a TiB is 1024 to the power 4. The units matter because BigQuery bills queries by bytes processed, not by rows returned or time taken.

BigQuery's console shows an estimate before a query runs, in a line such as "This query will process 1.2 GB when run".15 That figure is bytes, not money. Turning it into money means comparing it with the free allowance and multiplying anything above it by the rate on the pricing page.

BigQuery stores each column separately, so a query that names fewer columns processes fewer bytes. LIMIT does not reduce the bytes processed, because the columns are read before the rows are cut.16

A worked estimate for a small workload

Here is the estimate for a dataset about the size of a small project: 100,000 rows at roughly 200 bytes a row, queried 50 times a day, each query reading the whole table. Both figures are assumptions you would replace with real ones. The :.3f in an f-string prints a number with three decimal places.

GIB = 1024 ** 3
TIB = 1024 ** 4
FREE_STORAGE_GIB = 10
FREE_QUERY_TIB = 1

rows = 100000
bytes_per_row = 200
table_bytes = rows * bytes_per_row

queries_per_day = 50
scanned_per_month = table_bytes * queries_per_day * 30

storage_gib = table_bytes / GIB
query_tib = scanned_per_month / TIB
print(f"storage: {storage_gib:.3f} GiB of {FREE_STORAGE_GIB} free")
print(f"queries: {query_tib:.4f} TiB of {FREE_QUERY_TIB} free")


def billable_tib(used_tib):
    """TiB a month beyond the free allowance."""
    if used_tib <= FREE_QUERY_TIB:
        return 0
    return used_tib - FREE_QUERY_TIB


print("billable TiB this month:", billable_tib(query_tib))
print("billable TiB at 3 TiB:", billable_tib(3))
storage: 0.019 GiB of 10 free
queries: 0.0273 TiB of 1 free
billable TiB this month: 0
billable TiB at 3 TiB: 2

The table takes about 20 MB, under 1% of the free storage. A month of queries reads under 3% of the free query allowance, so this workload costs nothing on these meters. A team scanning 3 TiB a month would pay for 2 TiB at the on-demand rate on the pricing page.15

The weak spot of any estimate is the assumptions. Bytes per row is a guess until the data is loaded, and a busy dashboard can run far more than 50 queries a day. The console's byte estimate for a real query is the check that replaces the guess.

Labels: splitting a shared bill

One account often pays for many teams. Teams attach labels to resources so a shared bill can be split by project.

Takeaways
  • Pay-as-you-go has no commitment; committed use is cheaper but charged whether used or not.
  • The four meters are compute, storage, queries and egress.
  • BigQuery bills queries by bytes processed; fewer columns lower it, LIMIT does not.
  • An estimate multiplies size by frequency, then compares the result with the free allowance.
Say it in an interview

Cloud services are mostly pay-as-you-go: metered, with no commitment. If the load is steady I can commit for one or three years for a discount, knowing I pay even if I don't use it, and many products have free tiers. The meters are compute time, storage per GiB a month, queries such as BigQuery's bytes processed, and egress.

To estimate, I take rows times bytes per row for storage, multiply by queries a month for scanning, subtract the free allowance, and price the rest from the live pricing page.

Sandbox

The BigQuery sandbox and a first console query

The rest of the week puts the desk's history into BigQuery. Before that, two things have to work: the tools on your laptop and the sandbox project in your Google account.

Checking the setup from Git Bash

The weekend setup gave you three things.

  • A personal Google account.
  • A sandbox project named desk-<firstname><lastname>, in lower case.
  • The Google Cloud SDK, with gcloud for account and project settings and bq for BigQuery.

These notes were checked against SDK 591.0.

Three commands show whether the setup holds. Run them in Git Bash, one at a time.

gcloud --version
gcloud auth list
gcloud config get-value project

The first prints the SDK's version. The second should show your personal Google account as the active one. The third should print your project id in the shape desk-<id>.

Two gaps turn up most often. The first is no default project, which shows as an empty line from the third command; gcloud config set project desk-<id> fixes it. The second is running gcloud auth login but skipping gcloud auth application-default login. The second login is the one Python programs rely on, and Thursday's Python needs it; there is no key file to keep anywhere.

The last check runs a real query through bq. The flag --use_legacy_sql=false asks for GoogleSQL, the dialect these notes use, and the double quotes keep the whole statement as one argument.

bq query --use_legacy_sql=false "SELECT 1 AS ok"
+----+
| ok |
+----+
|  1 |
+----+

A table with one column named ok and the value 1 means the tools, the credentials and the project all work together.

The sandbox and its caps

The BigQuery sandbox needs no billing account and no card.25 It works inside the free allowances from step 6: 10 GiB of storage and 1 TiB of query processing each month.15 The console shows a sandbox banner while you work in it.

The cap that surprises people is the expiration. The sandbox applies a default 60-day expiration to tables and partitions, so anything you create is deleted 60 days later unless you change it.25 For a course that is plenty; for a project with old data it is a limit to plan around.

A first query in the console

Open BigQuery in the Google Cloud console, signed in with the same personal account and with your sandbox project selected. Google publishes some large tables for anyone to query, and one of them counts baby names registered in the United States. Type this into the query editor.

SELECT name, SUM(number) AS total
FROM `bigquery-public-data.usa_names.usa_1910_current`
GROUP BY name
ORDER BY total DESC
LIMIT 10;

Before you press Run, the editor's validator shows a line in the shape "This query will process N MB when run".15 After the run, the results tab lists ten names with their totals, largest first, and the Job information tab shows the bytes the query processed. Nothing is saved to your project.

The table name has three parts separated by dots: the project that publishes it, a named group of tables, and the table. The backquotes keep the dashed project name in one piece. Wednesday's page explains the middle part.

The estimate covers the two columns the query names, name and number. Removing LIMIT 10 would not change it, because the limit trims rows after the columns are read.16 Reading that line before every Run keeps a real project's bill predictable.

Takeaways
  • The auth list, the project value and one bq query prove the setup works.
  • The sandbox needs no card and gives 10 GiB of storage and 1 TiB of queries monthly.
  • Sandbox tables expire after 60 days unless the expiration is changed.
  • Read the validator's byte estimate before running any query.
Say it in an interview

Before I run a BigQuery query I read the validator line, "This query will process so many bytes". That's the amount on-demand pricing bills, so I narrow the columns if it's large. Adding a LIMIT doesn't lower it.

For learning I'd use the sandbox: no card, free storage and query allowances, and tables that expire after 60 days unless I change that.

Check yourself

Check yourself

Answer each question out loud, as you would to an interviewer, before you open it.

Define cloud computing in one sentence and name its five essential characteristics.

Cloud computing is on-demand network access to a shared pool of computing resources that a provider can hand out and take back quickly, with little management effort. The five characteristics are on-demand self-service, broad network access, resource pooling, rapid elasticity and measured service.

What is the difference between a region and an availability zone, and why spread a service over zones?

A region is a geographic area such as Toronto, northamerica-northeast2, and a zone is an isolated deployment area inside it with its own power and networking. Copies in several zones keep the service running when one zone fails; zones do not protect against a whole region's problem.

A colleague calls Cloud SQL "IaaS, because there's a MySQL server in it". What do you answer?

The test is what the team still manages, not what runs inside. Google patches, backs up and fails over Cloud SQL, and the team manages only schema, users and queries, so it is PaaS. MySQL on a Compute Engine virtual machine that the team patches would be IaaS.

What benefit does each of IaaS, PaaS and SaaS buy a team, and what does it give up?

IaaS removes buying hardware and gives machines in minutes, but the team still runs the OS and the database. PaaS adds patches, backups and failover done by the provider. SaaS removes installing and running anything. Each step up gives up control over versions, tuning and placement.

Name the four deployment models people ask about and a situation that suits each.

Public cloud suits a startup that wants to pay as it grows; private cloud suits a bank's core ledger kept under its own control. Hybrid suits a hospital that keeps records on site and bursts to the cloud at peaks. Multi-cloud suits a firm hedging against one provider's prices or outages.

A budget is set with an alert at 100%. The month's spending reaches 110%. What happened, and what is the plan for cost control?

The alert went out and the spending carried on, because a budget notifies and does not stop anything. Cost control combines budgets and alerts, labels that split the bill by project, a person who acts on the alert, and a regular review.

What are the four meters on a cloud bill, and how would you estimate a small BigQuery workload?

Compute, storage, queries and egress. Multiply rows by bytes per row for storage, multiply the bytes each query reads by the queries a month for processing, subtract the free 10 GiB of storage and 1 TiB of queries, and price what is left from the live pricing page.

List the common GCP services, then name the AWS and Azure equivalents of BigQuery and Cloud Storage.

Compute Engine, Cloud Storage, Cloud SQL, BigQuery, Cloud Run, Pub/Sub, Dataflow and IAM. BigQuery matches Amazon Redshift and Azure Synapse Analytics; Cloud Storage matches Amazon S3 and Azure Blob Storage.

Adding LIMIT 10 to a BigQuery query: does it lower the "This query will process" estimate? What does the sandbox add as a limit?

No. The estimate follows the columns read, and LIMIT trims rows after they are read; naming fewer columns lowers it. The sandbox caps storage and queries at the free allowances and deletes tables after 60 days unless the expiration is changed.
